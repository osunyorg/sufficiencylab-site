by Yamina Saheb

2026-10-22

body: >-


Empty
homes have become one of Europe's preferred climate solutions. Faced with
housing shortages, rising construction costs, and growing pressure to reduce
emissions, policymakers are increasingly turning their attention to what
already exists: empty flats above shops, offices that may never be fully
occupied again, and houses with more space than their residents use. This shift
is welcome. Reusing buildings generally requires fewer
materials , less energy, and less land than building new ones.

But
there is a risk in how this agenda is being framed. In many policy discussions,
the focus is no longer on sufficiency, but on the " better
use of existing buildings ". The phrase sounds sensible and
uncontroversial, yet it strips away much of what makes sufficiency a
transformative idea.

The
IPCC
identifies sufficiency as the first pillar of climate mitigation in the
buildings sector, ahead of efficiency improvements and renewable energy. Sufficiency is about " avoiding
the demand for energy, materials, land, water and other natural resources while
delivering wellbeing for all within planetary boundaries " . It
combines two objectives: a social floor below which nobody should fall, and an
ecological ceiling that society cannot collectively exceed.

The
idea of "better use" captures neither. Instead, it turns housing into
a productivity question: how can we generate more housing from the existing
stock?

This is essentially an efficiency approach applied to
buildings. And the benefits of efficiency improvements are often limited by rebound
effects . Bringing an empty building back into use does not automatically
reduce environmental pressures if new developments continue to spread across
farmland, demolitions continue, and living
space per person keeps increasing . In that case, reuse simply adds more
floor space to an already expanding system.

The
key question is therefore not whether an existing building can be reused, but
whether its reuse avoids the need for new construction. Reuse becomes a
sufficiency strategy only when it replaces new development rather than
accompanies it.   Three
questions need to be brought back to the centre of the European debate.

First,
what are the limits?

Today,
the environmental value of reuse is often measured against a hypothetical new
development. A reused building is considered successful if it performs better
than a newly built one. But this is a relative measure. Sufficiency
requires us to ask a different question: does housing remain within planetary
boundaries ?

How
much land can still be developed? How much carbon can still be emitted? How
many materials can still be extracted?

Europe
has committed to ending net land take , yet housing policies are rarely
evaluated according to their contribution to this objective. Success is still
largely measured by the amount of housing delivered. From a s ufficiency
perspective, housing policy should instead be judged by its ability to avoid
the need for new construction altogether.

 

 

Second,
wellbeing for whom?

Justice
sits at the heart of sufficiency . The environmental challenge is not simply
that Europe uses too much housing space and too many resources. It is that
housing resources are distributed profoundly unequally.

Discussions
about "under-occupied "
homes often focus on older people living alone in family houses. They are easy
to identify, highly visible in statistics, and often
have limited ability to resist public scrutiny . Yet, this focus raises an
uncomfortable question: w hy do housing policies so often start with those who
have a spare room rather than those who own multiple homes?

The
same cities that contain underused dwellings also contain overcrowded
flats, waiting lists for social housing, young adults unable to leave
their parents' home, and people experiencing homelessness. These realities are
connected. Under-occupation, overcrowding and homelessness are not separate
problems. They reflect the unequal distribution of access to housing.

A
sufficiency perspective therefore asks not only where space is unused, but who
lacks access to adequate space and who controls more than they need . This
shifts attention toward second homes that sit empty most of the year,
investment properties kept vacant, short-term
rental portfolios , and housing
held primarily as a financial asset rather than as a place to live.

This
distinction matters because climate transitions that fail to address
inequalities rarely gain legitimacy.  Asking
ordinary households to adjust while leaving speculative accumulation untouched
risks turning sufficiency into a burden for those with the least power and the
fewest alternatives.

The
question is not simply how to use housing more efficiently. It is how to ensure
that everyone has access to adequate and affordable housing before some
continue to have more housing than they need.

Third,
what counts as success?

What
we measure shapes what we value.

If
success is measured in square metres delivered, then the most visible and
resource-intensive interventions will always appear most successful: large
conversions, extensions, and redevelopment projects.

Less
material-intensive solutions tend to disappear. Home-sharing,
intergenerational living, cooperative housing, and housing exchanges often
avoid more demand than construction projects. They can also meet social needs
that conventional housing indicators rarely capture: companionship,
care, mutual support, and a sense of belonging .

Housing
is not only about providing physical space. It is also about creating the
conditions for security, participation, and social connection.

Policy
instruments must therefore be seen as part of a wider strategy rather than as
isolated measures. A vacancy tax, limits on land development, affordable
housing policies, and stronger tenant protections reinforce one another. Assessing
them individually misses how they work together to reduce demand and improve
wellbeing .

Underlying
all of these debates is a question that European
housing act does not ask: how much living space is needed to ensure a good
life for each of us?

Space
requirements are often treated as personal preferences. In reality, they are social
norms shaped by planning
rules, mortgage systems, housing markets, and building standards . Across
Europe, these expectations have increased steadily for decades.

As
long as those expectations remain unquestioned, gains achieved through reuse
are likely to be absorbed elsewhere. A square metre saved in one place can
easily reappear as a larger home, a second residence, or another housing
development.

Answering
the question of how much is needed to ensure a good life for each of us cannot be left to
markets alone. It is fundamentally a democratic question .

At
the same time, sufficiency cannot be pursued through surveillance. Monitoring people through
mobile phone data, social media activity, or other intrusive methods would
quickly erode public trust . Instead, r esidents, tenants, and local
communities must play a central role in deciding what constitutes wellbeing and
how housing resources should be shared .

None
of this is an argument against bringing empty homes back into use. On the
contrary, reuse should be a central part of housing and climate policy. But its
value does not lie in producing more housing supply. Its value lies in making
it possible to meet housing needs while building less, consuming fewer
resources and using less land.

The
question, however, is not only environmental. It is political. Who benefits
from the space that is freed? Who decides what counts for a good life? And whose
housing needs are prioritised?

Climate
justice requires more than lower emissions . It requires a fair distribution of
housing opportunities, resources and environmental burdens . Reusing empty homes
contributes to that goal only if it helps those who currently have too little
while placing limits on those having too much.

It
also requires ensuring that any housing brought back into use remains
affordable over time. Otherwise, public resources can end up subsidising the
transfer of vacant buildings into profitable assets, while housing insecurity
persists. Community land trusts, housing cooperatives, social housing providers
and anti-speculation safeguards are therefore not optional additions. They are
the mechanisms through which the benefits of reuse are shared rather than
captured. Without them, reuse may reduce emissions while leaving housing
inequalities untouched, or even worsening them .

Europe's
housing crisis and ecological crisis stem from the same belief: that every
shortage can be solved by producing more. Sufficiency starts from a different
principle. It asks how existing resources can be shared fairly before new
resources are extracted . Sufficiency asks not only how much housing society can build,
but how to ensure everyone has access to adequate and affordable home. Unless those questions
are addressed, the reuse of empty homes may improve efficiency, but it will not
deliver climate justice.
