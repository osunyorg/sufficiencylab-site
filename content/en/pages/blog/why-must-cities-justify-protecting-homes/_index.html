by Yamina Saheb

2026-09-11

body: >-


The Affordable
Housing Act , proposed by the European Commission, is presented as Europe's
answer to a housing crisis that now reaches deep into the middle class.

 

I naively assumed the Act would
address under-occupation, long-term vacancy, second homes and the accumulation
of residential property beyond what any household needs; that it would seek to
make already-built housing available to those who need it. It does none of
these things.

 

The Act does not limit how many
dwellings a person may own, nor does it require owners to demonstrate any
residential need for acquiring or retaining them. What it regulates instead is
the conduct of public authorities.

 

Articles 6 to 13 set out what a
municipality must establish before it may act: documented harm over at least
three preceding years; proof that no less restrictive measure would be equally
effective; automatic expiry after five years; publication of the supporting
evidence; and judicial review at every stage.

 

In practice, the Act requires public
authorities to justify protecting housing while never requiring owners to
justify withholding it . The question it asks is not why a dwelling stands
empty, is occupied only a few weeks a year, or is held primarily as a financial
asset. The question it asks is why a municipality wants to keep it a home.

 

At that point, the Act ceases to be
merely technical and becomes constitutional.

 

Under Article 12, a local measure
expires after five years and, where the required conditions are no longer
deemed to exist, the authority " shall withdraw" it. A decision
adopted by elected representatives can therefore be terminated by operation of
Union law rather than democratic choice. The three-year evidence requirement
excludes precisely the cases where early intervention is most effective: a sudden
tourism boom, for example, or a wave of investor purchases in a small housing
market.

 

The burden of assembling evidence,
publishing it and defending it in court is one that Paris or Amsterdam can
bear. A coastal town of forty thousand inhabitants may struggle to do so. The
right to act is formally equal but practically rationed by administrative
capacity.

 

Then there is the threshold.

 

A municipality may act only if its
price-to-income ratio reaches eight , a single figure that determines which
communities across Europe are eligible to protect their housing stock. Article
6(5) empowers the Commission to alter that threshold by delegated act.
Parliament and Council may object; they may not amend. T he scope of local
democratic authority over housing becomes, in effect, a parameter set by the
European executive.

The Commission's response is that
none of this is new. Cities are already constrained by the Treaty freedoms and
by the jurisprudence of the Court of Justice. The Act simply codifies those
constraints, removes an existing notification requirement and offers a
defensible methodology in place of uncertainty. That argument is not without
merit, which helps explain why many mayors welcomed the proposal.

 

But codification is never neutral .

 

It determines which justifications
count and which solutions remain visible. This Act recognises restrictions on
demand while leaving the mobilisation of empty and underused housing largely
outside the assessment.

 

Legal certainty for cities is not
the same thing as democratic authority for cities. Nor is this a drafting
failure.

 

The Act rests on Article 114 TFEU,
the internal market clause , because that is the only constitutional doorway
available. Through that doorway, housing enters Union law primarily as a
potential restriction on the free movement of services and capital. And
restrictions must be justified. They must be proportionate. They must be
temporary.

 

Meanwhile, housing as an asset , and
the appreciation of its value, benefits from the protection afforded by Article
63 TFEU o n the free movement of capital, which housing legislation cannot
fundamentally challenge. The expression " an open market economy with
free competition " appears repeatedly in the treaties as a
constitutional principle. No equivalent formula establishes a social floor
beneath which no one should fall, or a planetary ceiling beyond which economic
activity should not go.

 

Housing is not unique.

 

The same asymmetry appears wherever
Europe confronts the realities of a finite world. Cities must justify limits on
short-term rentals. Governments must justify restrictions on airport expansion.
Member States must justify interventions designed to reduce material or energy
consumption. The burden of proof consistently falls on those seeking to remain
within ecological limits rather than on the activities that push beyond them.

 

What the Affordable Housing Act
ultimately reveals is something larger than a housing problem.

 

It reveals a constitutional
settlement designed for an era in which Europe's central challenge was the
i ntegration of markets and the expansion of production and consumption . Today's
challenges are different. They concern the fair distribution of resources, the
protection of ecological systems and the provision of essential services for
all within planetary boundaries. Yet, the treaties continue to grant
constitutional priority to the logic of expansion.

The housing crisis is therefore not
merely a policy failure. It is evidence of a constitutional model reaching the
limits of its historical usefulness.

 

The objection is familiar: housing
is a national competence, and subsidiarity must be respected. But the real
question is not who decides. It is whether Europeans are currently allowed to
decide at all.

 

If a city wishes to prioritise
residents over investors, should it have to justify itself as an exception to
the market? If Europeans conclude that avoiding embodied emissions matters more
than continuously rising property values, should that decision be
constitutionally available? Today, the treaties answer these questions before
democratic debate even begins. Certain economic freedoms enjoy constitutional
protection. Housing does not. Nor, for that matter, do planetary boundaries.

 

The Affordable Housing Act begins
with a simple question: how can Europeans afford a home? In doing so, it
unintentionally raises a much larger one: what is the European Union for?

 

If the answer remains competition
and the expansion of markets, housing crises will become harder to resolve and
ecological overshoot harder to avoid. But if the answer is wellbeing, justice
and stewardship of a shared world; if the Union begins from the premise that
everyone should have enough for a good life and that no economic freedom should
override that objective, then treaty change is no longer an institutional
luxury. It becomes the next chapter of European integration.

 

The Affordable Housing Act starts
from the premise that a city must justify protecting homes. Perhaps it is time
to ask the opposite question.

 

Why should a city have to prove that
people need homes, while no one has to prove why homes should be treated as
assets?

 

A Union that asks cities to justify
housing people may eventually have to justify itself.
